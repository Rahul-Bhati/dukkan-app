## Intro

Large Language Models (LLMs) have increasingly been adopted for
discriminative tasks such as topic classification, sentiment analysis, stance
detection, and clinical risk prediction
Brown et al. (2020)
;
Cruickshank and Ng (2023)
;
Rezk et al. (2024)
. Unlike
traditional classifiers, which must be retrained whenever the label space or
task definition changes, LLMs can generalize to unseen tasks through prompting
Kojima et al. (2022)
, making them particularly attractive when task
definitions evolve rapidly. This flexibility has led to widespread deployment
of LLMs as zero-shot or few-shot discriminative models across domains such as
finance, healthcare, and large-scale content moderation
Naveed et al. (2023)
. However, this capability comes at substantial
computational and monetary cost. For example, inference with frontier-scale models can cost over
$20 per million tokens, and even lightweight models still incur
nontrivial cost when applied to large corpora
Erol et al. (2025)
.
For data at scale, such as millions of documents
in financial compliance screening and e-commerce
review classification, cumulative inference cost and latency quickly become
prohibitive, which makes direct LLM deployment impractical for accurate,
low cost discriminative workloads
Bai et al. (2024)
.
A natural solution to these limitations is to distill LLMs into smaller
models
Hinton et al. (2015)
. Early work primarily focused on
distilling large LLMs into smaller language models, preserving generative
capacity while reducing parameter count
Sanh et al. (2019)
;
Jiao et al. (2019)
;
Sun et al. (2020)
. While
effective, such approaches remain computationally expensive and often fail to
deliver order-of-magnitude savings in inference latency or cost
Wan et al. (2023)
.
In contrast, more recent research has shown that
LLM behavior can be distilled into task-specific discriminative models (e.g.,
multi-layer perceptrons, linear models, or support vector machines) that
reduce training and inference costs by orders of magnitude while maintaining
strong performance
Ho et al. (2022)
;
Bhat and Varma (2023)
.
These approaches typically treat the
LLM as an oracle annotator and use active learning to minimize the number of
LLM queries
Xia et al. (2025)
;
Xiang et al. (2025)
, selecting only the most
informative samples for labeling. The resulting objective is to train a
small discriminative student that approximates the final labels produced by
the LLM, achieving substantial financial and time savings suitable for
real-world deployment
Shridhar et al. (2022)
.
A key reason LLMs achieve strong performance on complex discriminative tasks
is their ability to generate and exploit explicit reasoning processes, such
as Chain-of-Thought, Tree-of-Thought, and Graph-of-Thought reasoning
Wei et al. (2022)
;
Yao et al. (2023a)
;
Yao et al. (2023b)
. These paradigms can be
viewed as structured, directed acyclic graphs of intermediate reasoning states
that enable test-time computation scaling and systematic exploration of
decision paths, leading to improved accuracy and robustness
Yang et al. (2023)
. A growing body of work has empirically shown that
eliciting intermediate reasoning substantially enhances LLM performance,
particularly on tasks requiring compositional or multi-factor judgment
Wei et al. (2022)
;
Kojima et al. (2022)
. Despite this, existing distillation
pipelines largely discard intermediate reasoning signals, training student
models solely on final outputs in an end-to-end fashion
Hinton et al. (2015)
. This practice results in sparse supervision,
obscures the structure of the decision process, reduces interpretability, and
limits the student’s ability to recover the teacher’s reasoning competence,
especially when distilled into simple discriminative architectures
Ho et al. (2022)
.
To approach teacher-level performance, a small discriminative model must
learn not only what prediction to make, but also how to arrive at it
Wei et al. (2022)
. This motivates distilling the reasoning process itself
rather than only final labels.
However, incorporating reasoning supervision
fundamentally complicates the active distillation pipeline. For sample
selection, reasoning introduces structured, multi-step dependencies:
different examples may expose weaknesses in different parts of the reasoning
process, making flat uncertainty or diversity criteria over final predictions
insufficient
Wei et al. (2022)
;
Yao et al. (2023a)
.
Selecting samples that
improve reasoning fidelity requires estimating, before the teacher is
queried, where in the reasoning the student is likely to err. For model retraining, reasoning supervision localizes errors
to specific intermediate components rather than the final output alone,
raising the challenge of attributing downstream loss to internal decision
modules and updating them efficiently without retraining the entire model
Huang and Chang (2022)
. These challenges affect both the efficiency and
stability of active distillation when reasoning is taken seriously.

To address these challenges, we propose Graph of Concept Predictors (GCP), a
reasoning-aware active distillation framework for small discriminative
models. As shown in Figure
1
, GCP explicitly represents the
teacher’s reasoning process as a structured, directed acyclic graph and uses
it to guide both sample selection and model retraining. To address sample
selection, GCP scores each text by uncertainty, gradient diversity, and
coverage at every concept node, weighted by centrality, rather than by
final-output uncertainty alone, targeting the reasoning steps where the
student is likely to err. To address retraining, GCP decomposes the
student into modular sub-components aligned with reasoning stages and
performs targeted retraining on the modules most responsible for downstream
errors. This design enables dense, interpretable supervision of reasoning
while preserving the efficiency advantages of small discriminative models,
making reasoning-aware distillation practical at scale.
We summarize our main contributions as follows:
(a) We proposed

(b) We designed a

effects.
(c) We introduced a

(d) We conducted
extensive experiments

dynamics.

## Method

Given a classification task, an unlabeled pool, and an LLM teacher with an
annotation budget, GCP trains a small student that predicts labels through
the teacher’s intermediate concepts. It consists of four components. First,
concept graph construction elicits the teacher’s reasoning as a directed
acyclic graph (DAG) of concepts and keeps only the parts that the data
support. Next, a Graph of Concept Predictors mirrors this graph with one
predictor per concept. Graph-aware acquisition then uses the student’s
concept-level predictions to choose texts for annotation, and finally
sub-module retraining updates only the predictors responsible for most of
the loss and freezes the rest. The central idea is that one concept graph indexes the
student’s modules, the teacher’s supervision, the acquisition scores, and the
retraining units. Figure
2
shows the loop.
